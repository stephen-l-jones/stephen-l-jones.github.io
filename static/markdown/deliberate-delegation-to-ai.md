##### By Stephen Jones, Ph.D.
###### Updated November 2026

THE RELEASE of GPT-4 in 2023 meant Boston Consulting Group (BCG) had to face AI. OpenAI’s model was a technical leap for knowledge work. It could handle long documents and conversations and even passed a simulated bar exam that its predecessor had failed. It could clearly help with management consulting too. But would it make BCG’s consultants better, or would it undermine the advice clients paid for?

BCG put the question to a test. Working with academic researchers, it enlisted 758 of its consultants and gave them assignments designed by senior BCG staff to mirror client work. Some consultants were randomly given GPT-4; others worked without it. Top performers would be noted to their supervisors, and the weakest would lose credit toward their bonuses⁠—giving the tasks real stakes.

Consultants assigned to one task benefited from AI. Those using it were 25% faster at developing new shoe ideas for a fictional footwear company. Their work was also rated 30% better.

But consultants assigned to a similar task did worse with AI. They had to advise a fictional clothing company’s CEO⁠ on whether to invest in its men’s, women’s, or kids’ line⁠, using sales data and interviews with its executives. The best answer hinged on subtle clues in the interviews. Consultants who used GPT-4 recommended the wrong investment nearly twice as often as those who didn’t, and those given additional AI training did even worse. What’s more, the incorrect AI-assisted memos were more convincing than the correct memos written without AI.

The [researchers concluded](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838) that AI has a “jagged technological frontier”⁠—an uneven boundary between tasks it handles well and tasks it doesn’t. Two tasks that seem equally hard to humans may sit on opposite sides of the frontier, and users may not know which side they’re on. Consequently, AI errors are often hard to detect, hiding within polished answers.

Describing this incongruity, one [well-known AI researcher](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/year-in-review-2025/) quipped that AI models are “at the same time a genius polymath and a confused and cognitively challenged grade schooler.”

This becomes particularly concerning when AI acts on its own. In 2025 Anthropic, the maker of Claude, tested how well an AI agent could [run a small self-service shop](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-1) for a month. The agent, nicknamed Claudius, ran the shop⁠—a refrigerator, a few baskets, and a self-checkout tablet in Anthropic’s San Francisco office. It chose what to stock, set prices, ordered inventory, and answered customers over Slack. Humans only handled the physical restocking.

Claudius proved easy to talk into discounts. When an employee questioned a 25% staff discount for a shop that only serves staff, it replied, “You make an excellent point!” It announced a plan to eliminate discount codes, then offered them again within days. When another employee jokingly asked for a tungsten cube, Claudius began stocking “specialty metal items” and sold them for less than it paid. Tungsten proved to be its biggest loser. Anthropic’s conclusion was unsparing: “If Anthropic were deciding today to expand into the in-office vending market, we would not hire Claudius.”

The errors documented in these tests may fade as AI improves, but the pattern behind them is likely to persist. AI will keep excelling at some tasks while stumbling on others that seem uncannily similar, and its errors will remain hidden in confident output.

This makes cognitive offloading doubly problematic. Users who casually offload thinking onto AI not only impoverish their learning, as discussed in [*ACER Critical Thinking Skills*](./acer-critical-thinking-skills), but also expose themselves to the full consequences of AI mistakes.

Judge Henry Wingate, a federal district judge in Mississippi, learned this the hard way. The US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, which oversees Mississippi’s federal courts, condemned an order of his, [calling it](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/25/25-60496-CV0.pdf) a “botch job.” It included nonexistent allegations, misidentified plaintiffs and defendants, and hallucinated citations. Judge Wingate blamed a law clerk’s use of AI for the slipshod order, but he faced the consequences himself. In an extraordinary move, the Fifth Circuit removed him from the case.

Judge Wingate’s experience raises a practical question. What should users delegate to AI, and how can they be confident in the result⁠—and avoid being blindsided by mistakes?

Responsible AI users delegate deliberately. And they retain ownership of their thinking and responsibility for what AI does on their behalf. Before handing work to AI, they choose what role it should play. While working with AI, they judge how far to trust its output, audit it for flaws, and decide when the work is done. In the end, they accept accountability for the result.

This third article in the *Critical Thinking in the Age of AI* series examines each of these steps.

## Choosing roles

Before handing work to AI, responsible users decide what role it should play. This choice shapes the quality of the work and what users learn. It’s one of the [evaluation skills](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#appendix) of critical thinking.

AI roles fall into two classes. Accelerators speed up work by supplying its substance⁠—the ideas, drafts, or actions that become the finished product. Amplifiers improve users’ own thinking by helping them understand, develop, and test their ideas.

The BCG consultants who used GPT-4 on the clothing-company task [exemplified both classes](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/26-036_e7d0e59a-904c-49f1-b610-56eb2bdfe6f9.pdf). Some consultants handed over the whole task, pasting the entire assignment, every interview, and the financial table into a single prompt, and accepted what came back. Nearly half of them didn’t change a word. As one consultant admitted, “I was feeling lazy. Frankly. I was like, how should I write 500 words now? I don’t want to write 500 words, I want GenAI to produce it.” These consultants treated AI purely as an accelerator of their work.

Others used AI sparingly. They asked it to help map the problem and suggest methods, then did the analysis themselves. They used AI mainly to polish drafts they had written. These consultants made the most accurate recommendations, and they deepened their expertise along the way. For this group, AI served chiefly as an amplifier of their own thinking.

Each class includes three roles.

| Role             | Purpose                   | Definition                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Accelerators** |                           |                                                                                                                                        |
| Brainstormer     | Widen your options        | Generates many ideas, themes, approaches, or solutions for you to select from and develop                                              |
| Producer         | Create deliverables       | Generates extended output on your behalf, such as drafts, analyses, summaries, or code                                                 |
| Agent            | Complete tasks            | Takes actions on your behalf, such as sending messages, scheduling meetings, filling in forms, or compiling information across sources |
| **Amplifiers**   |                           |                                                                                                                                        |
| Tutor            | Learn what you don’t know | Explains concepts, clarifies confusion, breaks work into manageable steps, and coaches you as you work                                 |
| Thinking partner | Develop your ideas        | Restates your thinking, asks questions, lays out options, and adds points to consider                                                  |
| Critic           | Test your thinking        | Examines your thinking from perspectives other than your own, exposing flaws in logic and evidence and gaps in what you’ve considered  |

Most BCG consultants used a mix of accelerator and amplifier roles, letting AI do much of the analysis but questioning it along the way, which many tasks call for.

### Accelerator roles

Accelerators save users time. As a brainstormer, AI generates ideas, themes, or approaches⁠—often more of them, and faster, than users could alone. As a producer, it drafts documents, analyses, summaries, or code, taking on work that would otherwise consume hours. As an agent, it acts on users’ behalf, sending messages, filling in forms, or compiling information across sources.

All three carry the same risk. Users who hand over their thinking along with the work learn little and can miss AI’s mistakes. They may settle on the first idea a brainstormer offers and never see what they might have come up with themselves. They may submit a producer’s draft and never fully understand it. That’s what the BCG consultants who handed AI the whole task did, and they built no new skills. And users may rely on an agent and not realize its mistakes until it’s too late⁠—as with Claudius, who handed out discounts and sold tungsten cubes at a loss. The risk grows with the autonomy users grant.

None of this makes accelerators the wrong choice. When a task is routine and users can check the output, an accelerator frees them to focus on work that needs their judgment.

### Amplifier roles

Amplifiers improve users’ thinking. As a tutor, AI helps users learn what they don’t know. It teaches concepts, clarifies confusion, breaks work into manageable steps, and coaches users as they work. It responds to users’ attempts with a hint or a next step rather than an answer, so users build understanding. In an [experiment with high school math students](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), unrestricted access to GPT-4 harmed learning, while a version designed to give hints largely prevented the harm. Users know the tutoring has worked when they can explain the material without AI. They activate the role by asking AI to unpack a concept, to give a hint when they’re stuck, or to quiz them on what they’ve learned.

As a thinking partner, AI helps users develop their own ideas. It restates rough thinking so users can see it more clearly, asks questions that draw out what they mean, lays out options, and adds points to consider. It is most useful when users have a view to develop or a decision to make. For example, AI can make the strongest case for each side, helping them see what considerations matter most. Even when AI adds points of its own, users decide which to keep. Users activate the role by sharing their rough thinking and asking AI to restate their ideas, inviting clarifying questions, or requesting points they haven’t considered.

As a critic, AI tests users’ thinking. It examines their reasoning from other perspectives, exposing flaws in logic and evidence and gaps in what users have considered. A critic needs something to test, so the role works best once users have a position. Users also have to ask for criticism explicitly. AI tends to tell users what they want to hear, so those who ask “Is this good?” often get reassurance. Users activate the role by sharing their position and asking AI for the strongest counterargument, requesting the response of a skeptical executive or an affected employee, or inviting critique of their evidence.

### How to choose

Three questions help users choose a role.

*Is this use of AI allowed?* Users answer to others who may limit how they can use AI. Course policies, employer guidelines, and confidentiality concerns can prohibit it entirely. In 2023, Samsung engineers pasted internal source code into ChatGPT, where it would be stored on servers Samsung couldn’t control. Within weeks, Samsung banned generative AI tools on company devices and warned employees that breaking the rule could get them fired. Many limits go unstated, so users may not know what’s allowed. When unsure, users can ask, “Would I be comfortable explaining my AI use to those I answer to?” If the answer is no, the right choice is probably no AI.

*Is the goal the output or the learning?* Often it is both, so users weigh which matters more, and for which parts of the work. The people they answer to usually signal which. Managers expect reliable output that employees can explain and defend. Instructors assign work as a vehicle for learning. Still, users can easily misjudge the balance by, for example, focusing on output when the intent is learning. Students in the high school math experiment made this misjudgment. Many used GPT-4 to get answers to practice problems, and they scored 48% higher on them than students without AI. But once AI was taken away for the exam⁠—the true measure of their learning⁠—they scored 17% lower.

When output matters more, accelerators work well. When learning is the goal, amplifiers are best. Choosing them takes [intellectual courage](./critical-thinking-dispositions#courage), since amplifiers require productive struggle. Which amplifier fits depends on the stage of users’ thinking. A tutor helps when users lack the knowledge to begin, a thinking partner once they have a rough view to develop, and a critic when they have a position to test.

Others’ expectations and users’ own goals can conflict. A manager’s deadline may favor a producer, while an employee’s own development favors a tutor or thinking partner. Neither is always right, so users have to weigh the trade-off deliberately.

*On this task, are mistakes costly or hard to catch?* Users rarely know in advance which side of the frontier a task falls on. But they can judge what a mistake would cost and whether they could catch one. Mistakes in routine tasks are usually cheap and easy to spot. In a task like the BCG segment decision, a mistake is costly, and a polished answer can hide it.

When the risk of hidden mistakes is low, users can safely rely on accelerators. When it is high, they have two options. They can do more of the thinking themselves, using amplifiers for the judgment-heavy parts of the work. Or they can still use an accelerator but carefully check its output against independent sources. For brainstorming, that source can be users’ own list of ideas, generated before they see AI’s. For an agent, high risk also calls for closer oversight, such as approving its actions before it takes them.

Most tasks call for more than one role, and the three questions shape how users combine them. Consider two memos written by Ruth, a marketing analyst at a regional coffee chain.

In the first, she has one day to recommend which perk to cut from the chain’s loyalty program. She designed the program and knows which perks are popular. She asks AI to brainstorm options, decides on a recommendation, and outlines her key points. AI drafts the memo from her outline. She checks the draft against sources, edits it, and asks AI how readers might object.

In the second, her boss asks whether the chain should sell bottled coffee in grocery stores, a market she knows little about. She lists her own ideas before asking AI for more. She has AI explain concepts she doesn’t understand. She shares her rough view and asks AI to restate it more clearly. She finalizes her recommendation and drafts the memo herself. Then she asks AI to highlight unclear passages and critique her arguments, and revises the memo.

The three questions guide the roles Ruth chooses. AI is allowed in both cases, so all roles are available. For the loyalty-program memo, the output matters most because of the tight deadline, and she knows the topic well enough to catch mistakes. So she uses the brainstormer and producer roles to supply most of the substance. For the bottled-coffee memo, learning matters most, since she needs to understand the market to defend her recommendation. She also isn’t confident she could catch AI’s mistakes on an unfamiliar topic. So she relies on the tutor and thinking partner roles, and the brainstormer role only after making her own list. For both memos, she uses the critic role as a final check.

## Working with AI

Whatever role AI plays, users must judge how much to trust its output, audit it for flaws, and decide when the work is done.

### Building trust

Users build trust in AI through experience. As they check AI’s output, they learn how reliable it is. The goal is [calibrated trust](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392)⁠—trust that matches what AI can actually do. Too little trust leads users to give up on AI after a mistake, even when working with it could produce a better result than working alone. Too much leads them to accept AI’s output without checking it, even when it’s flawed.

Giving up on AI too soon is often prompted by algorithm aversion, described in [*Critical Thinking Dispositions*](./critical-thinking-dispositions#skepticism). It leads users to judge AI against perfection. AI should be judged instead against a realistic alternative⁠—often doing the work without AI. The question is which approach leaves fewer flaws in the finished work. That depends on how often AI’s output is flawed on a given task and how many of those flaws users catch.

Suppose Ravi, an accounts payable clerk, checks 100 invoices a week against purchase orders and flags any mismatches. He tries an AI tool that automates the work, and in the first week, he discovers a mismatch the tool missed. He’s tempted to drop it. But unbeknownst to Ravi, the tool misses only about four mismatches a week, and his reviews catch half of those, so about two reach the finished work. Working alone, he misses about six as his attention fades. Dropping the tool would triple his errors.

To avoid trusting AI too much, careful users extend trust systematically. They follow a basic practice: start small and scale up. They give AI limited tasks and check the results. As AI proves dependable, they expand its responsibilities, eventually combining small tasks into larger ones. This guards against flaws, which become [more common as tasks grow longer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499).

This practice applies to each new kind of task, since trust earned on one task doesn’t justify trust on another (given the jagged frontier). It also applies when circumstances change, such as when the consequences of mistakes rise. A manager may trust AI to build slides for her team meetings but personally check every slide it makes for an executive meeting. A new AI model calls for rechecking too, since its strengths and weaknesses may shift.

Growing trust carries its own risk. In 2018, a self-driving Uber test car struck and killed a pedestrian in Arizona. The car’s safety driver was supposed to watch the road and take over when needed. Instead, she had likely been streaming *The Voice* on her phone, and looked away from the road for at least five seconds before the crash. Federal investigators blamed Uber in part for failing to guard against “automation complacency”⁠—the tendency to stop monitoring a system that seems to work. Knowledge workers show a milder version. [A survey showed](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) that those more confident in AI reported putting less effort into scrutinizing its output. Some of that is appropriate, since a reliable tool deserves less scrutiny. But spot-checking is how users know whether continued trust is warranted. Those who stop checking lose the evidence they need to keep trust calibrated.

### Auditing

Catching flaws and keeping trust calibrated depend on continued auditing. Auditing requires checking AI’s output against an independent source, such as the original data, a reliable reference, or someone who knows the field.

Users may be tempted to ask AI to check its own work. But AI’s attempts at self-correction can make things worse. When models reviewed their answers to reasoning problems, they were more likely to [turn a right answer wrong](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798) than to fix a wrong one. Self-correction works mainly when [reliable outside feedback is available](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00713).

Using one AI to check another helps little when both run on the same underlying model. In Project Vend, an AI “CEO” added to supervise Claudius [shared many of its blind spots](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-2). A model from a different company is a better check, but different models often [make the same errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962), especially the most capable ones.

Auditing also depends on what users know. Expertise reveals flaws hidden inside fluent answers. But people tend to overestimate how well they understand a subject, a bias called the [illusion of explanatory depth](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1). Fluent AI output can deepen the illusion, making users feel they understand a subject better than they do. Careful users don’t take their understanding for granted, instead showing [intellectual humility](./critical-thinking-dispositions#humility). They test their understanding by trying to answer key questions about the subject without AI, which is one of the [reflection skills](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#appendix) of critical thinking.

Consider Gabriela, a purchasing manager, who shows this humility. She asks AI to summarize a supplier contract. The summary reads well, and she feels she understands it. But when she sets it aside and asks herself a basic question⁠—who pays if a shipment arrives damaged⁠—she can’t answer it, and realizes her legal knowledge is thinner than she thought. She checks the clause against the contract’s text and asks a colleague in legal to review it.

Knowing AI’s weaknesses also tells users when to audit most closely.

- **Specific details.** Models are trained in ways that [reward guessing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664) over admitting uncertainty, so hallucinations concentrate in names, figures, dates, and citations. They are most common on knowledge that [appears rarely in training data](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/kandpal23a), such as a niche industry or a specific regulation. Careful users check these details against original sources.

- **Agreeableness.** AI tends to be sycophantic. When users say they like their own argument, AI’s feedback on it [becomes more favorable](https://www.anthropic.com/research/towards-understanding-sycophancy-in-language-models). When challenged, models often [abandon answers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08840) that were right. Careful users ask for criticism before revealing their own view.

- **Typical output.** Models generate likely responses, so AI-assisted work tends to converge. Stories written with AI-generated ideas were rated more creative but [more similar to one another](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290). When novelty matters, careful users generate their own ideas first and compare them with AI’s.

- **Missing context.** AI can’t reason about what it doesn’t know, such as a project’s background, an audience’s expectations, or which priorities matter most. It fills gaps with plausible assumptions, so its output can miss the mark even when nothing in it is wrong. Careful users state their context and priorities and check whether an answer’s priorities are their own.

As trust grows, auditing changes form. For Ravi, the accounts payable clerk, that means reviewing every invoice his AI tool checks at first, then spot-checking a sample as the tool proves reliable. When a spot-check turns up a mismatch the tool missed, he rechecks the rest of that week’s invoices.

Experienced users of Anthropic’s coding agent show the same pattern. They approve fewer of its actions in advance but [interrupt it more often](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy), monitoring its work rather than approving each step.

### Deciding when the work is done

Eventually, users must decide when the work is done⁠—when it meets their standards. Careful users avoid stopping prematurely, which is tempting when fatigue sets in, a deadline approaches, or the work has a semblance of completeness but hasn’t been scrutinized. When the work needs revision, users can keep iterating with AI, take over the work themselves, or start over.

Careful users keep iterating with AI when more information or clearer direction can fix any remaining flaws, or when interacting with AI is helping them work out what they want. A user unsure how to frame a proposal might go several rounds with AI before the right framing emerges.

They take over the work when iterating with AI becomes inefficient⁠—when each round fixes one problem but creates another, or when a fix is faster to make than to describe. They also take over when what remains is a judgment only they can make, or when they would learn more by finishing the work themselves. Relying on AI round after round can [undercut that learning](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills).

They start over when AI has latched onto a flawed framing. Each new round builds on the original response, so corrections may not fully undo a poor start. A fresh conversation with a better first request can get further than another round of fixes.

## Accepting accountability

Once the work is done, users have to stand behind it. Accountability does not transfer to AI. Judge Wingate blamed a law clerk’s use of AI, but the order carried his name, and the consequences were his. The same holds for a student submitting an assignment or an employee sending a memo.

Accepting accountability means taking responsibility for the work and its consequences, whoever or whatever produced it. [Intellectual integrity](./critical-thinking-dispositions#integrity) is the disposition that supports it. Taking responsibility requires understanding the work, so responsible users make sure they can explain and defend it without AI. Defending it means walking through the evidence and reasoning behind any claim that’s questioned. They prepare by imagining a skeptical reviewer or by asking AI to play one.

Accepting accountability also means being honest about AI’s part. Principled users avoid the [two failures of integrity](./critical-thinking-dispositions#integrity) described in the previous article. They don’t overclaim by presenting AI’s work as their own thinking. And they don’t underclaim by disowning AI’s work when it goes wrong. Instead, they accept criticism without deflecting it, disclose AI’s role where it’s expected, and stand behind what they submit.

With agents, it is harder to tell who is accountable, because users grant them autonomy. An agent can act without anyone reviewing each step, so its mistakes may have no obvious owner. The problem grows when organizations embed agents in their workflows. Control spreads across many people, and responsibility becomes harder still to trace. If an agentic system fails, blame can fall on the nearest human⁠—the one in the [moral crumple zone](https://doi.org/10.17351/ests2019.260)⁠—even when that person has little control over the system. Users who set up agents, and those who design and manage these systems, need to draw clear lines of accountability in advance, matched to who controls what.

Choosing roles, judging AI’s output, and accepting accountability are how users retain ownership of their thinking and responsibility for what AI does on their behalf.
