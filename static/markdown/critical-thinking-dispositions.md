###### Updated November 2026

BENJAMIN FRANKLIN became one of the most celebrated scientists of his age for his observations on electricity, including his famous kite-and-key experiment. Yet he never received scientific training. Instead, he drew on his insatiable curiosity, his skeptical attitude, and the humility to change his mind when evidence countered his opinion.

On one transatlantic voyage, Franklin noticed something odd. Two ships in the fleet had smooth wakes, while the wakes behind every other ship were choppy. He pointed this out to the captain, who reckoned the cooks had just emptied their greasy dishwater over the side. Franklin dismissed the captain’s explanation, though he had no alternative to offer. He kept musing on the oddity. Later, an experienced mariner told him that Atlantic fishermen used oil to smooth the water.

Franklin decided to conduct his own experiment. While standing on the bank of a large pond whipped rough by the wind, he dropped a little oil on the waves. To [his surprise](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-20-02-0250), “the Oil tho’ not more than a Tea Spoonful produced an instant Calm, over a Space several yards square.” It kept spreading until perhaps half an acre of the pond lay “as smooth as a Looking Glass.” His experiment backed the captain’s claim.

This intrigued Franklin. He took to carrying a little oil in the hollow joint of his bamboo walking cane so he could repeat the experiment wherever he found open water. He documented his experiments, including failures, in a long letter the Royal Society published. His observations influenced modern studies of surface chemistry.

![Benjamin Franklin](../img/benjamin-franklin.webp#right)

Franklin’s fame landed him atop a special royal commission while he was serving as America’s ambassador to France. At the time, Paris was in the grip of animal magnetism. Charles-Nicolas d’Eslon, a student of the German physician who “discovered” an invisible fluid that flowed through all living things, was packing treatment rooms with fashionable patients. Disease, d’Eslon claimed, arose when the fluid became blocked. A trained practitioner like himself could restore its flow. Patients sat around a large oak tub with iron rods protruding from it. They pressed the rods against ailing body parts to conduct the magnetism, aided by music from a nearby piano. Many fell into convulsions⁠—termed a “crisis”⁠—that could last for hours. Some of France’s leading physicians and intellectuals became converts.

The Franklin Commission [approached the phenomenon](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/78413) “with that philosophic doubt which ought always to accompany enquiry.” The commissioners first had themselves magnetized. They felt nothing. Given that others reacted strongly, the commissioners formed a hypothesis: the effects came not from a fluid but from the patients’ expectations. They set out to test it.

The best-known test took place in Franklin’s orchard. D’Eslon chose a boy of about 12 whom he knew to be highly susceptible, and magnetized a single apricot tree while the boy waited inside. The boy was then blindfolded, led to four other trees⁠—none of them magnetized⁠—and told to embrace each for two minutes. At the first he began sweating and coughing. At the second he felt dazed and his head ached. At the third the symptoms grew worse. At the fourth, he fainted and his limbs went rigid.

Other tests cast further doubt. A woman, told that d’Eslon was magnetizing her from behind a closed door, went into a full crisis within three minutes, though no one was there. Another chatted cheerfully for 30 minutes, unaware that a commissioner was magnetizing her on the other side of a paper screen. When the same commissioner then did it openly, she fell into a crisis in three minutes.

The Franklin Commission concluded that the effects were real, but imagination alone produced them.

The intellectual dispositions Franklin displayed⁠—curiosity, skepticism, and humility⁠—are essential for critical thinkers, as are integrity and courage. Knowing how to think critically isn’t enough. Good reasoning also requires a committed heart⁠—the motivation, attitude, and ethical orientation to pursue truth.

This second article in the *Critical Thinking in the Age of AI* series explains five dispositions critical thinkers strive to develop.

## Curiosity

Percy Spencer had a knack for turning oddities into innovations. In 1945 he was standing beside an active radar set at Raytheon, a defense contractor, when he noticed that the candy bar in his pocket had melted. World War II radars detected distant ships and aircraft by beaming out microwaves (a high-frequency form of radio wave) and timing the echoes that bounced back. The melted candy bar hinted to Spencer that microwaves might be able to cook food. So he began to investigate.

Like Franklin, Spencer had little formal schooling to draw on. He left school around the fifth grade to work in a factory. But he had a growth mindset: he believed he could learn whatever he needed through persistent effort. At 16 he volunteered to help wire a local paper mill for electricity, though he knew nothing about the subject. He taught himself at night, from textbooks and by experimenting. In the Navy he worked through calculus, physics, and metallurgy while standing watch. By World War II he ran Raytheon’s tube division. There he found a way to mass-produce the magnetron (the tube at the heart of radar) instead of machining each one by hand. Under his method, production rose from 17 tubes a day to 2,600.

The melted candy bar was not the first oddity Spencer had refused to ignore. Earlier in his career he noticed a small leak in a photoelectric tube, a device that turns light into an electric current. Such tubes were normally discarded as defective. Spencer investigated instead and found that the leak made the tube more sensitive to light⁠—an insight that helped pave the way for television cameras.

To test the culinary power of microwaves, Spencer held popcorn kernels near the radar’s magnetron, and they popped across the lab. Next he cut a hole in the side of a kettle, put an egg inside, and aimed the microwaves through the hole. The egg exploded in the face of a skeptical colleague who was peering in.

Raytheon patented Spencer’s method of cooking with microwaves and paid him $2, its standard bonus for an employee patent. The first commercial Radarange, introduced in 1947, stood nearly six feet tall and weighed about 750 pounds. Twenty years later, Raytheon began selling a countertop model, and by 1997 nine in ten American households owned a microwave oven.

Curiosity is an urge for discovery⁠—a desire to grasp what one doesn’t understand. Curious thinkers are drawn to puzzles, oddities, and unexplained phenomena, which focus their attention. They ask more questions, notice more, and keep probing after a plausible answer appears.

Curiosity is particularly important in the age of AI. Users can easily offload cognitive work onto AI, which circumvents learning. Curious thinkers resist that temptation. They use AI to deepen their domain knowledge.

Curiosity especially supports analysis and creation, two of the cognitive modes in [*ACER Critical Thinking Skills*](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#appendix). Curious thinkers find interesting patterns and notice anomalies when dissecting a problem, and they treat an oddity as an invitation to pursue an explanation. For Spencer the oddity was a melted candy bar; for Franklin it was two smooth wakes.

## Skepticism

In 1999 Harry Markopolos, an options specialist at an investment firm, was asked by his bosses to create an investment product that could compete with Bernie Madoff’s fund. Madoff’s fund had delivered steady gains of 1 to 2% a month for years, with almost no losing months. Madoff was a Wall Street pillar and a former chairman of the Nasdaq stock market. Markopolos’ task was figuring out how Madoff did it.

Madoff said he used a strategy called split-strike conversion. He bought a basket of large companies’ stocks, then used options (contracts to buy or sell at a set price) to cap his gains and limit his losses. Markopolos knew the strategy well. It could earn decent returns in some markets but not all, and it could not produce a decade of near-perfect steadiness.

The more Markopolos looked, the less the numbers fit. Madoff claimed to own stocks that mirrored the market, so his returns should have risen and fallen with it. But they barely moved with the market at all. And the options Madoff needed to protect his stocks would have outnumbered all the options of that kind in existence. Markopolos tried repeatedly to replicate the returns and could not. He concluded that the returns were fabricated.

In May 2000 he submitted his analysis to the Securities and Exchange Commission, which regulates investment funds. Nothing happened. He resubmitted in 2001. Nothing happened. He kept digging. With two colleagues he gathered information on who was investing with Madoff and what they were being told, and he shared his doubts with financial journalists. In October 2005 he sent the SEC a memo titled “The World’s Largest Hedge Fund Is a Fraud,” listing 29 red flags. The agency looked into Madoff and found no fraud. Markopolos kept contacting the SEC for three more years. Frustrated, he would later [tell Congress](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-111hhrg48673/html/CHRG-111hhrg48673.htm) that the SEC “continues to roar like a mouse and bite like a flea.”

Markopolos’ skepticism was ultimately vindicated. In December 2008 Madoff confessed. He had orchestrated the largest Ponzi scheme in history, paying earlier investors with money from newer ones. The scheme’s unraveling led to about $65 billion of reported losses.

Skeptics hold a questioning attitude and point out claims that don’t pass critical review⁠—such as Madoff’s impossible returns, or claims of animal magnetism. Skepticism is not cynicism or a habit of doubting everything. It is a refusal to accept a claim until the evidence and reasoning warrant it. Skeptics are not hard to convince; they are hard to convince without good reasons. That is why Franklin’s commission did not dismiss animal magnetism outright⁠—it tested it.

Skepticism matters when using AI. AI models tend to be sycophantic⁠—agreeing with their users, praising their ideas, affirming their reasoning, and backing their first guesses. This makes AI an easy source of false confirmation. Skeptics treat AI’s agreement with caution, checking whether an answer rests on evidence or simply echoes their own view.

But skeptics avoid unfounded distrust too. Both humans and AI err, but research suggests that people forgive human errors while abandoning algorithms that make similar ones⁠—even when the algorithm is more accurate. This is called [algorithm aversion](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033). Skeptics believe AI output, like any claim, in proportion to the evidence and reasoning behind it.

Skepticism undergirds [evaluation skills](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#evaluation). A questioning attitude puts those skills to work. Skeptics weigh the evidence for and against a claim, look for hidden assumptions, and test whether its conclusion follows. And they don’t overlook premises, since an argument built on faulty information fails no matter how sound its logic. Markopolos did all of this. He questioned whether Madoff could have made the options trades he claimed, and he compared Madoff’s returns with the market’s. That’s when he knew that the returns couldn’t be real.

## Humility

In 1854 cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district in central London. Whole families fell ill, and in 10 days more than 500 people living near the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street were dead. Many residents fled, leaving rows of shuttered houses.

Henry Whitehead, the curate of St Luke’s Church, stayed. He spent the outbreak going from house to house among parishioners he knew by name, sitting with the sick and bringing them food and last rites.

Most physicians at the time blamed miasma⁠—the theory that disease spread through foul air rising from filth and rot. John Snow, a local doctor, believed instead that cholera spread through contaminated water and that this outbreak came from the public pump on Broad Street. He persuaded the parish authorities to remove the pump’s handle.

![Henry Whitehead](../img/henry-whitehead.webp#right)

Whitehead thought Snow was wrong. He had good reasons. Many survivors told him they had recovered after drinking the pump’s water. Whitehead had drunk it himself during the outbreak and stayed well. Snow gave Whitehead a copy of his newly published book, *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera*. Whitehead read it and wrote back, laying out his objections. Then he set out to test Snow’s theory, expecting to refute it.

Whitehead had an advantage Snow lacked: the people of Broad Street trusted him. He tracked down families who had fled and asked where they got their water, who drank it, and when they fell ill. He gathered information on more than half of the street’s residents.

The evidence countered Whitehead’s opinion. House by house, the people who drank from the pump were the ones who fell ill. Even a pattern that had puzzled him began to make sense. The elderly, many of whom lived alone with no one to fetch water from the pump, had largely been spared. Instead of exonerating the pump, the evidence condemned it. He changed his mind and started working to prove Snow right.

Snow’s theory left an open question: how had the well become contaminated in the first place? Whitehead found the answer in the record of local deaths. A five-month-old girl on Broad Street had fallen ill just before the outbreak began. Her mother had soaked the baby’s soiled diapers in buckets of water and emptied them into a cesspool in front of the house, a few feet from the well. When the site was dug up, authorities found that the cesspool’s lining was decayed and leaking toward the pump.

Whitehead, who had set out to defend the pump, became Snow’s most important ally.

Intellectual humility is recognizing the limits of one’s knowledge and the possibility of being wrong. It is skepticism turned inward⁠—holding one’s own beliefs to the same standard of evidence applied to others’ claims. Humility is not self-doubt or timidity. Humble thinkers still form views and act on them. They simply hold them provisionally⁠—as claims to be further tested. Franklin did this when he tested the captain’s explanation and found it held. Whitehead did it at greater cost. He publicly set out to show the pump was safe, then let his own evidence prove him wrong.

Humility guards against AI’s sycophancy. AI often backs down when a user pushes back. Humble thinkers don’t push AI toward agreement. Instead, they turn it against their own views. They ask it to critique their reasoning or make the strongest case against their position. Then they weigh the merits of its answer.

Humility strengthens evaluation. People naturally evaluate their own arguments charitably⁠—a tendency called [myside bias](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#evaluation)⁠—but humble thinkers resist it. They consider evidence against their position and steelman views they disagree with before critiquing them. Humility also drives [reflection](./acer-critical-thinking-skills#reflection). Humble thinkers calibrate their confidence to the strength of their evidence. They ask whether they even know enough to reason about a topic. The same question applies to AI. When an answer lies beyond their expertise, humble thinkers check it against other sources.

## Integrity

Roger Boisjoly had seen it coming. He was an engineer at Morton Thiokol, the aerospace company that built the rocket boosters for NASA’s space shuttles. Rubber O-rings sealed the joints between booster sections, and on earlier flights they had scorched and eroded⁠—particularly in cold weather. In July 1985 Boisjoly warned Thiokol’s vice president of engineering, Bob Lund, in a memo. Without a fix, [he wrote](https://www.npr.org/2026/01/25/g-s1-106940/40-years-after-challenger), the result could be “a catastrophe of the highest order⁠—loss of human life.”

On January 27, 1986, the night before Challenger’s launch, the forecast called for subfreezing temperatures. Thiokol’s engineers recommended against launching below 53 degrees, the coldest temperature of any previous launch. NASA pushed back. One manager called the data inconclusive and [reportedly asked](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch5.htm), “My God, Thiokol, when do you want me to launch, next April?”

Thiokol’s managers conferred privately. Boisjoly and a colleague made their case again, but the managers didn’t listen. Thiokol’s senior vice president asked whether he was the only one who wanted to fly. He told Lund to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat. Lund changed his vote, and Thiokol told NASA the launch could proceed.

NASA wanted the reversal in writing. Allan McDonald, who directed Thiokol’s booster program, refused to sign. He told NASA he would not want to explain to a board of inquiry why they had launched. His boss signed instead.

The next morning, Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, killing all seven astronauts aboard. The engineers watched in horror.

Boisjoly and McDonald could have kept quiet. Two weeks after the disaster, McDonald sat in a closed commission hearing as a NASA official testified that Thiokol had raised concerns but approved the launch. McDonald raised his hand and told the commission that Thiokol had recommended against launching. The chairman asked him to come down to the floor and repeat it. Boisjoly testified too and handed over his memos. Thiokol demoted McDonald and reassigned Boisjoly, drawing objections from the commission and Congress. Boisjoly, shunned by colleagues, eventually resigned.

Neither man hid behind having been overruled. Boisjoly asked himself whether he could have done more. McDonald told his daughter, “I feel like it’s my fault.” Years later, paraphrasing a columnist, he [put it this way](https://www.npr.org/2021/03/07/974534021/remembering-allan-mcdonald-he-refused-to-approve-challenger-launch-exposed-cover): “Regret for things we did is tempered by time. But regret for things we did not do is inconsolable.”

Intellectual integrity is honesty about what one knows and ownership of what one produces. Boisjoly’s memo frankly stated the O-ring risk, and McDonald told the commission what had actually happened the night before the launch. Principled thinkers own their cognitive work and its products. McDonald refused to put his name on a recommendation he didn’t support. Principled thinkers also accept responsibility for the consequences of their reasoning, including what they failed to do.

Integrity protects against two common lapses when working with AI. Overclaiming is presenting AI’s work as one’s own thinking. Underclaiming is disowning AI-produced work when it goes wrong, treating errors or failures as the tool’s fault. Principled thinkers avoid both. They disclose how they used AI, and they stand behind whatever they submit, whoever drafted it. Before putting their name on AI-assisted work, they make sure they can explain and defend it without AI’s help.

Integrity also guards creation and evaluation. When building arguments, principled thinkers claim no more than their evidence supports and report the limits of their work, failures included. In evaluation, they resist motivated reasoning⁠—the temptation to work backward to justify a desired conclusion. Here, Thiokol’s managers failed. They wanted to keep NASA, a major customer, happy, so they reversed their position. Only then did they assemble a rationale.

## Courage

Frances Kelsey joined the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 1960. One of her first assignments was to review an application to sell a sedative called Kevadon, submitted by the William S. Merrell Company. Its active ingredient was thalidomide, which had been sold in West Germany since 1957 and was on the market in more than 20 countries. Pregnant women also took it for morning sickness. Merrell expected approval to be routine.

But Kelsey wasn’t satisfied. The application rested on testimonials from doctors rather than clinical data. She requested safety data. Merrell resubmitted with more doctors’ endorsements but never the evidence she wanted. Each time, Kelsey found the application incomplete and asked again. Frustrated, Merrell pressed her to speed things up.

![Frances Kelsey](../img/frances-kelsey.webp#right)

In February 1961 she read a letter in the *British Medical Journal* reporting peripheral neuritis⁠—painful, sometimes permanent nerve damage in the hands and feet⁠—in patients taking thalidomide. Merrell hadn’t told the FDA. When pressed, the company blamed poor diet. But the report worried Kelsey. Her earlier research had shown that some drugs cross the placenta from mother to fetus. A drug that could damage adults’ nerves might do far worse to a developing baby. She asked Merrell to prove the drug was safe in pregnancy. Merrell offered to add a warning label instead. A label wasn’t enough for Kelsey. She still wanted the data.

The pressure mounted. Merrell’s representative contacted her repeatedly and complained to her superiors. In September 1961 the company asked to finalize its labeling so the drug could be on shelves by Christmas. Kelsey refused again.

Two months later, a German pediatrician linked thalidomide to an epidemic of babies born with severely malformed or missing limbs. Thousands of children were affected worldwide. Merrell withdrew its application in March 1962. Because Kelsey had kept the drug off American shelves, only a small number of such births occurred in the United States. In August 1962, President Kennedy gave her the President’s Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service.

Intellectual courage is accepting the conclusions that evidence and reasoning lead to, even when those conclusions come at a cost. Kelsey’s did. Holding up a drug sold in more than 20 countries meant defying a company, its endorsing doctors, and a widespread belief that the drug was safe. Courage is not mere stubbornness. Kelsey’s stand rested on sound reasoning. She asked only that Merrell show the drug was safe, and the evidence never came.

AI tests the courage to bear the cost of thinking. Using AI eases the hard work of producing well-reasoned ideas. But when learning is the goal, courageous thinkers lean into the difficulty, working through problems instead of handing them over to AI. Educators call this productive struggle⁠—the effortful and often uncomfortable thinking that builds real understanding.

The courage to struggle goes hand in hand with reflection. Reflective thinkers notice the temptation to shirk cognitive work, and with courage they keep working. They also notice when pressure from others is bending their judgment. With courage, they hold their ground. Kelsey faced such pressure for more than a year. She upheld her standard and delivered an unwelcome verdict again and again. Reflective thinkers also recognize when their own approach isn’t working. With courage, they bear the cost of changing course.

## The heart of a critical thinker

These dispositions form the heart of a critical thinker. But can they be learned? They might seem like fixed traits⁠—some people are simply curious, humble, or brave. Research suggests otherwise. Critical thinking dispositions [improve with instruction and effort](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063), especially when learners tackle authentic problems, discuss them, and receive mentoring.

Instruction can [spark curiosity](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/nurturing-curiosity-enhance-learning-turkey). Believing that intelligence can grow [boosts intellectual humility](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/something-we-could-use-little-more). And practice with feedback can [sharpen skepticism](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/12324).

The people featured in this article reasoned well because they brought such heart to their thinking. They kept asking questions, doubted claims that lacked warrant, admitted when they were wrong, owned their work, and bore the cost of following the evidence. Those who practice and strengthen the same habits develop as whole critical thinkers, in mind and heart.
